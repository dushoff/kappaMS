\section{Introduction}

Real outbreaks have significant heterogeneity in the number of secondary cases -- including superspreading, marked by a small fraction of individuals causing a disproportionately large fraction of realized transmission \cite{lloydsmith2005superspreading, woolhouse1997heterogeneities}. %Superspreading can arise from several distinct factors, each of which affects outbreak dynamics differently \cite{kuylen2022different,wallace2025hotspot}.
Superspreading can help explain why epidemics take off in some situations and not in others, despite apparently similar contexts \cite{lemieux2021phylogenetic, endo2020estimating}. Leveraging knowledge of super spreading can make control efforts more effective \cite{garske2008effect, nielsen2023counterintuitive, sneppen2021overdispersion}. It is especially important for disease dynamics and control strategies at the beginning of outbreaks  \cite{goyal2022multiscale}. Correspondingly, theoretical treatment of superspreading emphasizes individual-level transmission patterns, often while assuming a fully susceptible population \cite{lloydsmith2005superspreading,james2007event}. Theoretical analysis of superspreading has focused on models that link observed secondary case counts to a continuous random variable, the \emph{individual reproductive number}, via a Poisson sampling process. Canonically, the individual reproductive number is modeled as a gamma distribution, giving rise to negative binomial secondary case distributions \cite{lloydsmith2005superspreading}.
In this way, variation arising from the stochastic transmission process is conceptually separated from \textit{intrinsic heterogeneity} in expected infectiousness that may emerge from potential differences in biology or behaviors between individuals \cite{chen2021heterogeneity, althouse2020superspreading,hamner2020high}.

However, these models assume a constant pool of susceptible individuals and do not account for disease dynamics beyond the very initial stages of an outbreak. Over longer timescales, the susceptible depletion and replenishment drive population-scale disease dynamics. Changes in the size of susceptible population changes the statistics of the contact processes that lead to successful disease transmission. As a result, variation in realized secondary cases arises between early and late infectors, even when the underlying individual reproductive number distribution is identical. In practice, many deterministic epidemiological models also include \emph{explicit heterogeneity}, for example by adding compartments for different demographic or geographic groups \cite{schenzle1984agestructured}, which can have different average rates of mixing, transmission, or recovery. When implemented in terms of individual events, the inclusion of explicit heterogeneity reproduces superspreading in linear compartmental models \cite{meehan2023replicating}. Despite shared theory motivating models that highlight individual-level transmission, and those that emphasize population-level transmission dynamics, a gap persists in how each model framework treats individual-level transmission heterogeneity. 

The canonical SIR (Susceptible-Infected-Removed) model uses ordinary differential equations (ODEs) to describe disease dynamics. It assumes constant, uniform infectivity and recovery times across all individuals during the infectious stage \cite{Hethcote2000, Kermack1927}. Although it appears homogeneous, even the canonical SIR model has two sources of \emph{emergent heterogeneity.} The changing effective reproductive number arising from susceptible dynamics, drives variation between fractions of the population infected at different times; and the exponentially distributed recovery times assumed by the ODE drive variation in the number of infections attributable to fractions of the population infected at a given time. In other words, even the fully deterministic SIR model predicts variation in the expected number of secondary cases.

Here we delineate sources of individual variation in transmission inscribed in simple dynamical models. We trace how these sources compose into observed or observable heterogeneity in different settings, for example contrasting how heterogeneity emerges in dynamical models versus linearized, or branching-process models. We begin by revisiting early findings that intrinsic heterogeneity (e.g., due to differences in the basic reproductive number, $\mathcal{R}_0$ ) drives differences in variation in the secondary case distribution. However, when accounting for the feedback of transmission on disease dynamics, we find that emergent heterogeneity is an invariant across a full SIR outbreak and independent of differences in intrinsic heterogeneity. As we show, expected differences in superspreading may be a consequence, in part, of focusing on early rather than the full scope of outbreaks.  Throughout, we highlight the understudied but critically important role of disease dynamics \textit{per se} in generating individual-level heterogeneity. We aim to spur the development of theory and methods that better integrate heterogeneity and dynamics, ultimately facilitating feedback between data on incidence and individual-level transmission chains, mechanistic model specification, identification, validation, and intervention design.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Heterogeneity in the secondary case distribution in the linearized SIR model}

The early outbreak phase of an S(usceptible)-I(nfectious)-R(emoved) epidemic is modeled using a linearized SIR model, which assumes that the pool of susceptible individuals is very large and does not change appreciably at the outset of an epidemic. The early dynamics of the epidemic can be characterized by a transmission rate and an infectious period. Demographic stochasticity generates heterogeneity in this model despite the lack of explicit accounting of individual-level variation in transmission rates and infectious periods. The model implicitly assumes that the infectious period follows an exponential distribution which results in an exponentially distributed expected infectiousness  (or \emph{individual reproductive number}) with mean $\mu = \mathcal{R}_0$ and dispersion $\kappa = 1$. Infectious individuals spread the disease by coming into contact with susceptible (and other infectious individuals), which is modelled as a Poisson process. The resulting distribution of secondary cases (i.e., the number of new cases caused by a single infectious individual over their infectious lifetime) is geometric and has a mean equal to $\mathcal{R}_0$. Thus, the expected infectious and secondary case distributions are characterized by $\mathcal{R}_0$ (Figure \Fref{ls}a).

The heterogeneity in the secondary case distribution means that a large fraction of the initial secondary cases are generated by only a few infectors. Due to the Poisson contact process, the inequality curve for the realized secondary case distribution deviates from the inequality curve of the exponentially distributed expected infectiousness, in an $\mathcal{R}_0$ dependent manner (\Fref{ls}b). As $\mathcal{R}_0$~ increases, the inequality observed in the secondary case distribution decreases and approaches the expected infectiousness inequality curve (\Fref{ls}b). This heterogeneity arises from the implicit underlying differences in expected infectiousness and the stochasticity of the transmission process. \subsection{Heterogeneity in secondary cases over an epidemic is independent of $\mathcal{R}_0$ - simulation} 
Next we consider the impact of depletion of susceptible population during outbreaks on secondary case distributions? We simulated stochastic SIR epidemics with different $\mathcal{R}_0$ values in a population of 10000 individuals. For each infected individual, we recorded the number of secondary cases they generated during the outbreak. Panel (a) of Figure 2 shows realized distributions of secondary cases caused by individual infectors over the entire outbreaks. Unlike in the linearized scenario shown in Figure 1, where the susceptible pool remains fully populated, the secondary case distributions, computed across the entire outbreak, are indistinguishable across a broad range of the key parameter $\mathcal{R}_0$ (compare panels (a) of Figure 1 and Figure 2). This result is unexpected. In the absence of susceptiple depletion, heterogeneity is expected to be larger for smaller values of $\mathcal{R}_0$ as shown in Figure 1b. However, faster and larger dynamical changes in susecptible depletion during the epidemic occur when $\mathcal{R}_0$ is large (Figure S1), which drives larger variation in the expected infectiousness distribution and, hence, secondary-case distribution.  The observed stochastically realized secondary case distribution shown in Figure 2 includes multiple notable features. First, the mean of the secondary case distribution is  approximately 1. Because we are considering a single outbreak seeded by single initial case, then every case must have its origin within the outbreak. 
Second, the variance of the secondary case distribution is equal to 1, consistent with the emergence of what appears to be an exponential distribution in the secondary case distribution considered across an epidemic independent of $\mathcal{R}_0$.

\subsection{Heterogeneity in secondary cases over an epidemic is independent of $\mathcal{R}_0$ - Proof}
We set out to explore the basis for the finding that both the mean and variance of secondary cases in the stochastically realized SIR model is invariant with respect to changes in $\mathcal{R}_0$. To do so, consider the deterministic ODE-based SIR model:
\begin{align}
    \begin{split}
        \frac{dx}{dt} &= - \mathcal{R}_0 x(t)y(t), \\
        \frac{dy}{dt} &= \mathcal{R}_0 x(t)y(t) - y(t), \\
    \end{split}
\end{align}
where $x(t)$ is the proportion of susceptible individuals at time $t$, $y(t)$ is the proportion of infectious individuals at time $t$, $t$ is time measured in units of the mean infectious duration and $\mathcal{R}_0$ is the basic reproduction number.

To calculate the mean and variance of the secondary cases, we must track infections and the infectors that caused those infections. While individual infectors and cases cannot be tracked in this deterministic SIR model, we can track cohorts of infectors and the cases caused by them. By calculating the weighted moments of the secondary cases per case caused by each cohort, we can obtain the heterogeneity of the secondary cases per case over the epidemic. 

A cohort, $c_{\tau_1,\tau_2}$, is defined as the fraction of infectors that become infectious at time $\tau_1$ and stop being infectious at time $\tau_2$. The weight of the cohort, $w(\tau_1,\tau_2)$ is simply the number of infectors in the cohort. The number of infectors who become infectious at time $\tau_1$ is $i(\tau_1) = \mathcal{R_0}x(\tau_1)y(\tau_1)$. This quantity, $i(\tau_1)$ is also called the incidence of infection, or the number of new cases that appear at time $\tau_1$. Only a fraction of these infectors remain infectious till time $\tau_2$. If $f(.)$ denotes the probability density function of the infectious period, then
\begin{equation}
    w(\tau_1,\tau_2) = i(\tau_1)f(\tau_2-\tau_1)
\end{equation}

Now that we have the cohort weights, we need to calculate the number of cases per case caused by the cohort. The total number of cases caused by the cohort $c_{\tau_1,\tau_2}$ over the course of the epidemic is 
\begin{equation}
    \hat{C}(\tau_1, \tau_2) = \int_{\tau_1}^{\tau_2} \mathcal{R}_0 x(t') w(\tau_1,\tau_2)dt' = \mathcal{R}_0 w(\tau_1, \tau_2) \int_{\tau_1}^{\tau_2} x(t')dt'
\end{equation}
Then the secondary cases per case caused by cohort $c_{\tau_1,\tau_2}$ is
\begin{equation}
    C(\tau_1,\tau_2) = \frac{\hat{C}(\tau_1,\tau_2)}{w(\tau_1,\tau_2)} = \mathcal{R}_0 \int_{\tau_1}^{\tau_2} x(t')dt' 
\end{equation}

Now that we have the number of secondary cases per case and weight for each cohort, we can calculate the moments of the secondary cases per case distribution. The $k^{\text{th}}$ weighted raw moment of the secondary cases per case distribution, $C_k$ is
\begin{equation}
    C_k = \iint\limits_{0 < \tau_1 \leq \tau_2 < \infty}  d\tau_1 d\tau_2 \; w(\tau_1,\tau_2) (C(\tau_1 ,\tau_2))^k,
\end{equation}

With this definition of the raw moments, the assertion that the heterogeneity of the secondary case per case distribution over the entire epidemic is invariant is equivalent to:
\begin{equation}
    \kappa = \frac{C_0 C_2}{C_1^2} - 1 = 1 \label{eq:target_main}
\end{equation}

The detailed calculation of the moments and the proof can be found in (\tg{(refer to the appropriate section)}). We note that the result follows from two assumptions: (a) the distribution of the infectious duration is memoryless, and (b) the initial infectious cohort is infinitesimal, i.e., $y(t = 0) \rightarrow 0$. The first assumption is true for the canonical SIR model where the duration of infectious periods is exponentially distributed. The second assumption is generally true when considering large naive populations in which a very small number of infectious individuals are introduced. Intuitively, $C_0$ is the total number of cases over the course of the epidemic and $C_1$ is the total number of \textit{secondary} cases over the course of the epidemic. When the initial population of infectious individuals is infinitesimally small, all the cases can be attributed as secondary cases of some infectious individual who became infectious during the course of the epidemic and thus $C_0 = C_1 = Z$. (\tg{(Is there an intuitive explanation for why C2 is what it is?)}). However,  if the initial proportion of infectious individuals is not infinitesimally small, this equality no longer holds. The difference between $C_0$ and $C_1$ quantifies the size of the initial infectious cohort and $C_2$ deviates from $2Z$  with a deviation proportional to the size of the initial infectious population (refer to the SI section).


\subsection{Balance between within- and between-cohort components of the variance in secondary cases explains why heterogeneity in secondary cases is independent of $\mathcal{R}_0$}

To understand why the heterogeneity in secondary cases over the course of the epidemic is independent of $\mathcal{R}_0$, we split the heterogeneity into two components: a within-cohort component and a between-cohort component. A cohort refers to a group of individuals that become infectious at the same time (TG: make vertical slices in Figure 3a to mark cohorts). Individuals within a cohort have access to the same susceptible pool but have differences in their secondary case counts as a result of their exponentially distribution infectious periods. Thus, the within-cohort variance can be attributed to inherent individual-level differences in infectious periods. On the other hand, cohorts that arise at different times have realized differences in the number of secondary cases due to differences in the size of the susceptible pools that they have access to. It is important to note that both early and late cohorts have the same distributions of infectious periods and expected infectiousness but due to the depletion of the susceptible pool over time (Point to figure 3b, susceptibles over time), later cohorts, on average, have much lower realized secondary cases than earlier cohorts. Thus, the between-cohort variance can be attributed to susceptible depletion. 

Numerical calculation of the within- and between-cohort components of the variance in secondary cases using the ODE-based SIR model (see methods for details of the calculation) shows that as $\mathcal{R}_0$ increases, the between-cohort component of the variance increases and the within-cohort component of the variances decreases (Ref figure 2b, which should be changed to an area plot of the variance for many more values of $\mathcal{R}_0$). However, the sum of the two components, remains constant across $\mathcal{R}_0$.
As $\mathcal{R}_0$ increases, the variance in the size of infectious cohorts (fig 3a) and well as the speed and severity of susceptible depletion also increases (fig 3b). As a result of the susceptible depletion, the mean number of secondary cases also decreases rapidly across cohorts (fig 4b), with earlier cohorts having a much higher mean of secondary cases compared to later cohorts. Further, the decline in the mean number of secondary cases across cohorts becomes sharper as $\mathcal{R}_0$ increases. As a result, the between-cohort variance in the number of secondary cases increases with $\mathcal{R}_0$. 

The within-cohort variance, on the other hand, decreases as $\mathcal{R}_0$ increases (fig 2b). Since the mean number of secondary cases varies widely between early and late cohorts, we look at the within-cohort dispersion in secondary cases to better understand how within-cohort heterogeneity changes between early vs late cohorts for an epidemic with a given $\mathcal{R}_0$, and how the total contribution of within-cohort variance changes as a function of $\mathcal{R}_0$. For a given $\mathcal{R}_0$, early cohorts have high dispersion in secondary cases, driven by access to a large susceptible pool (fig 4c). As the epidemic progresses, the size of the cohorts increases (fig 4a), the susceptible pool depletes (fig 3b) and the within-cohort dispersion in secondary cases decreases (fig 4c). The within-cohort dispersion is minimum for the largest cohort of infectors. The dispersion increases again for later cohorts as the cohort size decreases, this time driven by the shrinking within-cohort mean in the number of secondary cases. As $\mathcal{R}_0$ increases, so does the reduction in dispersion as well as the size of the largest cohort. As a result, the cohort size weighted contribution of within-cohort variance towards the total variance decreases as the $\mathcal{R}_0$ increases. 

Together, the decrease in the within-cohort variances balances the increase in the between-cohort variance, leading to the total variances in secondary cases being independent of $\mathcal{R}_0$. This change in the within- and between-cohort variances is driven by susceptible depletion.

\section{Discussion}
 Quantifying heterogeneity in secondary-case distributions helps better understand how epidemics unfold and how they can be contained.
 Although there have been efforts to both quantitatively describe individual-level heterogeneity in contact rates \cite{woolhouse1997heterogeneities} and to parameterize biologically realistic secondary case distributions, abstracted from the mechanism \cite{lloydsmith2005superspreading}, these efforts have been largely separate from the construction and evaluation of the compartmental models that enable epidemic forecasting and intervention design (but see \cite{meehan2023replicating}).
  Here, we expand the bridge between individual- and population perspectives of 
  %disease spread
  the heterogeneity by studying epidemiologically-relevant cohorts, those infected at the same time.

 We observed that 
 early in the outbreak, mean and variance of the expected infectiousness vary by $\mathcal{R}_0$, so do those of secondary-case distributions.
  However, when computed across the entire outbreak,  these quantities become independent of $\mathcal{R}_0$.
  We indeed prove that, provided a negligible fraction of initial infectors, both mean and variance are equal to one, regardless of $\mathcal{R}_0$.
Disentangling the within- and between-cohort variances reveals that in epidemics with large $\mathcal{R}_0$, unlike those with smaller $\mathcal{R}_0$, the between-cohort variance dominates the within-cohort variance.

In real-time analyses, $\kappa$, used as a measure of heterogeneity, remains below one.

% Similar to how reproduction numbers ($R_\mathrm{effective}$ or $\mathcal{R}_0$) serve as common currency by which to measure and compare models and epidemics, our contribution invites parallel treatment of variation about those mean quantities.

There are some limitations to our work. 
The underlying model is based on a closed, well-mixed population.
It is not clear how heterogeneity in mixing propensity might impact the independence of heterogeneity in expected infectiousness from $\mathcal{R}_0$~and, hence,  alter $\kappa$.
Working with SIR models implicitly implies an exponential infectious period. 
Investigating the impact of infectious period distribution on the heterogeneity in expected infectiousness is left for future work.

In SIR-type epidemics, the effective reproduction number declines as the susceptible population becomes depleted. Consequently, if surveillance begins after the initial transmission generations, estimates of secondary-cases will represent the reduced effective reproduction number rather than $\mathcal{R}_0$ \cite{pellis}. As a result, delayed surveillance may yield substantially lower estimates, especially when $\mathcal{R}_0$~is large, due to rapid depletion of the susceptible pool.
