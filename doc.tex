\section{Introduction}

Real outbreaks have significant heterogeneity in the number of secondary cases -- including \emph{superspreading,} marked by a small fraction of individuals causing a disproportionately large fraction of realized transmission \cite{lloydsmith2005superspreading, woolhouse1997heterogeneities}.
%Superspreading can arise from several distinct factors, each of which affects outbreak dynamics differently \cite{kuylen2022different,wallace2025hotspot}.
 Superspreading can help explain why epidemics take off in some times and places but not others, despite apparently similar contexts \cite{lemieux2021phylogenetic, endo2020estimating}; and leveraging knowledge of superspreading can make control efforts more effective \cite{garske2008effect, nielsen2023counterintuitive, sneppen2021overdispersion}. 

Superspreading is most salient for disease dynamics and control strategies at the beginning of outbreaks \cite{goyal2022multiscale} \comm{Check this citation}. 
Correspondingly, theoretical treatment of superspreading emphasizes individual-level transmission patterns, often while assuming a fully susceptible population \cite{lloydsmith2005superspreading,james2007event}.
Theoretical analysis of superspreading has focused on models that link observed secondary case counts to a continuous random variable, the \emph{individual reproductive number,}  via a Poisson sampling process.
 Canonically, the individual reproductive number is modeled as a gamma distribution, giving rise to negative binomial secondary case distributions \cite{lloydsmith2005superspreading}.
In this way, variation from the stochastic process of successful transmission is conceptually separated from a more intrinsic individual-level heterogeneity [in expected infectiousness] that may emerge from a plethora of potential differences in biology, contact patterns, or behaviors between individuals \cite{chen2021heterogeneity, althouse2020superspreading,hamner2020high}.

Over longer timescales, the processes of susceptible depletion and replenishment, which are elegantly captured in mechanistic compartmental models, drive population-scale disease dynamics. Despite shared theory motivating models that highlight individual-level transmission, and those that emphasize population-level transmission dynamics, a gap persists in how each model framework treats individual-level transmission heterogeneity. 

The canonical SIR (Susceptible-Infected-Removed) model is based on ordinary differential equations (ODEs). It assumes constant, uniform infectivity and recovery across all individuals during the infectious stage \cite{Hethcote2000, Kermack1927}. 
Although single-infective-class compartmental models seem homogeneous, even the canonical SIR model has two sources of \emph{emergent heterogeneity.}
The changing effective reproductive number arising from susceptible dynamics, drives variation between fractions of the population infected at different times; and the exponentially distributed recovery times assumed by the ODE, drive variation in the number of infections attributable to fractions of the population infected at a given time.
In other words, even fully deterministic ODE models predict variation around basic reproductive number \Ro, the average number of secondary cases caused by a case in a fully susceptible population, in the expected secondary cases.

In practice, many epidemiological modelers also include \emph{explicit heterogeneity}, for example by adding compartments for different demographic or geographic groups \cite{schenzle1984agestructured}, which can have different average rates of mixing, transmission, or recovery.
Explicit heterogeneity reproduces superspreading in linear compartmental models \cite{meehan2023replicating}.

Here we delineate these \ag{is "these" necessary?} different sources of individual variation in transmission inscribed in simple dynamical models. We trace how these sources compose into observed or observable heterogeneity in different settings, for example contrasting how heterogeneity emerges in dynamical models versus linearized, or branching-process models. Throughout, we highlight the understudied but critically important role of disease dynamics \textit{per se} in generating individual-level heterogeneity. 
We aim to spur the development of theory and methods that better integrate heterogeneity and dynamics, ultimately facilitating feedback between data on incidence and individual-level transmission chains, mechanistic model specification, identification, and validation, and intervention design.

\section{Results}
Demographic stochasticity can generate emergent heterogeneity even in the absence of explicit differences in individual-level rates.
In outbreaks modeled using the linearized SIR compartmental models (with susceptible, infected, and removed compartments), this heterogeneity can be quantitatively characterized.
In these models, infectious period follows an exponential distribution, resulting in an exponentially distributed expected infectiousness (or \emph{individual reproductive number}) with $\kappa = 1$.
Consequently, the secondary-case distribution (or \emph{offspring distribution}), modeled as a Poisson mixture of the expected infectiousness, follows a geometric distribution with dispersion $\kappa$ (see Box).

Under a stochastic SIR model, where the number of secondary cases arises from  Poisson draws from expected infectiousness,
 inequality in secondary cases--that is, the relationship between the fraction of new infections and the fraction of infectors--depends on the mean, whereas inequality in expected infectiousness does not (\Fref{ls}, panel b).

As \Ro~becomes smaller, an increasing fraction of infected individuals generate no secondary infections, so the individuals who do transmit make up a smaller fraction of all infectors. However, the distributions plotted in \Fref{ls} neglect the depletion of the susceptible population which occurs in real outbreak.
To what extent does the incorporation of changes in the susceptible pool affect these distributions?
To address this, we simulated simple, stochastic SIR epidemics with different \Ro~values in a population of $10000$ individuals.
For each infected individual, we recorded the number of secondary cases they generated during the outbreak. 
Panel (a) of \Fref{RcAverage} shows realized distributions of secondary cases caused by individual infectors over the entire outbreaks.
Unlike in the linearized scenario, where the susceptible pool is fully populated (\Fref{ls}, panel a), the secondary case distributions remain indistinguishable across a broad range of the key parameter \Ro.
This result is unexpected. Faster and larger dynamical changes occur when \Ro~is large (\Fref{SIRsim}), which would typically be expected to correspond to larger variation in the expected infectiousness distribution  and, hence, secondary-case distribution.

To clarify these counterintuitive observations, we considered a standardized deterministic SIR model in which the time is measured in units of the mean infectious period.
We examined how heterogeneity evolves in epidemiologically relevant cohorts, defined as groups infected simultaneously, over the course of the outbreak.
A large \Ro~initially generates significant expected infectiousness. Later, rapid depletion of susceptible individuals reduces the expected infectiousness.
In contrast, a small \Ro~initially causes smaller expected infectiousness, but the slower susceptible exhaustion allows for considerable expected infectiousness (\Fref{Cohort}).
We then split the total variance in expected infectiousness over the course of the outbreak into between- and within-cohort components. We found that epidemics with larger \Ro\ have larger between-cohort variation, as expected. However, this is balanced by smaller within-cohort variation (\Fref{RcAverage}, panel b). 
We can in fact prove that the mean and variance of expected infectiousness, conditioned on the entire outbreak, is equal to one, regardless of the value of \Ro~(Section \ref{sec_proof}). 
Thus, the dispersion $\kappa$ is also one.

We then investigated how expected infectiousness would evolve over time?
%In particular, how cohorts evolve over time and how the outbreak appears when observed at specific time points.
To do so, for different values of \Ro, we simulated an outbreak using the standardized deterministic SIR model and evaluated it at several cutoff (time) points.
At each cutoff point, we calculated heterogeneity in expected infectiousness between cases generated by that time.
In the early stage of the outbreak, observed heterogeneity was lower than in the linearized model or the full outbreak, and it varied by \Ro.
From the beginning, in epidemics with large \Ro, the between-cohort variation was the main driver of heterogeneity. 
By the end of the outbreak, heterogeneity in expected infectiousness among different \Ro~ values became indistinguishable (\fref{Naive}).

We also examined an idealized scenario in which, for each cutoff point, we computed heterogeneity in expected infectiousness over the entire outbreak, but considering those cohorts that had become infectious up to that time.
In epidemics with lower \Ro, the mean and standard deviation of expected infectiousness for early-infected cohorts almost matched those from the linearized model, i.e., mean and standard deviation equal \Ro~ (\fref{timeCutoff}).
Including cohorts infected later, but before the outbreak peak, reduced variability as measured by $\kappa$. Including cohorts infected after the peak increased variability.
By the end of the outbreak, all epidemics with different \Ro~agreed on the heterogeneity in expected infectiousness, measured by $\kappa$.
Regardless of \Ro, within-cohort variation primarily drove heterogeneity among early-infected cohorts.
However, after the peak, the susceptible depletion reduced the share of within-cohort variation, a reduction that was more pronounced in epidemics with large \Ro.

\section{Discussion}
 Quantifying heterogeneity in secondary-case distributions helps better understand how epidemics unfold and how they can be contained.
 Although there have been efforts to both quantitatively describe individual-level heterogeneity in contact rates \cite{woolhouse1997heterogeneities} and to parameterize biologically realistic secondary case distributions, abstracted from the mechanism \cite{lloydsmith2005superspreading}, these efforts have been largely separate from the construction and evaluation of the compartmental models that enable epidemic forecasting and intervention design (but see \cite{meehan2023replicating}).
  Here, we expand the bridge between individual- and population perspectives of 
  %disease spread
  the heterogeneity by studying epidemiologically-relevant cohorts, those infected at the same time.

%[x,y,z] x, y, z might consist of dynamical consideration of superspreading, outbreak characteristics of compartmental models, opportunity for model identifyability or validation with secondary case data, etc.


%We rigorously contrast emergent heterogeneity from compartmental models and the observed heterogeneity secondary case distributions from stochastic realizations of those models.

To do so, we first describe  a statistic, $\kappa$, for quantifying the emergent heterogeneity entailed in the SIR models and their stochastic realizations.
 Next,  we compare the emergent heterogeneity from deterministic ODE SIR models to the dispersion in secondary case distributions resulted from their stochastic realizations. 
 Finally, we consider how different time frames dictate different views of emergent heterogeneity, and how expected infectiousness evolves through time in epidemics of different strengths.
 
 We observed that 
 early in the outbreak, mean and variance of the expected infectiousness vary by \Ro, so do those of secondary-case distributions.
  However, when conditioned over the entire outbreak, they become independent of \Ro.
  We indeed prove that, provided a negligible fraction of initial infectors, both mean and variance are equal to one, regardless of \Ro.
Disentangling the within- and between-cohort variances reveals that in epidemics with large \Ro, unlike those with smaller \Ro, the between-cohort variance dominates the within-cohort variance.

In real-time analyses, $\kappa$, used as a measure of heterogeneity, remains below one.

% Similar to how reproduction numbers ($R_\mathrm{effective}$ or \Ro) serve as common currency by which to measure and compare models and epidemics, our contribution invites parallel treatment of variation about those mean quantities.

There are some limitations to our work. 
The underlying model is based on a closed, well-mixed population.
It is not clear how heterogeneity in mixing propensity might impact the independence of heterogeneity in expected infectiousness from \Ro~and, hence,  alter $\kappa$.
Working with SIR models implicitly implies an exponential infectious period. 
Investigating the impact of infectious period distribution on the heterogeneity in expected infectiousness is left for future work.
