Real outbreaks have significant heterogeneity in the number of secondary cases -- including superspreading, marked by a small fraction of individuals causing a disproportionately large fraction of realized transmission \cite{lloydsmith2005superspreading, woolhouse1997heterogeneities}. %Superspreading can arise from several distinct factors, each of which affects outbreak dynamics differently \cite{kuylen2022different,wallace2025hotspot}.
Superspreading can help explain why epidemics take off in some situations and not in others, despite apparently similar contexts \cite{lemieux2021phylogenetic, endo2020estimating}. Leveraging knowledge of superspreading can make control efforts more effective \cite{garske2008effect, nielsen2023counterintuitive, sneppen2021overdispersion}. It is especially important for disease dynamics and control strategies at the beginning of outbreaks  \cite{goyal2022multiscale}. Correspondingly, theoretical treatment of superspreading emphasizes individual-level transmission patterns, often while assuming a fully susceptible population, \cite{lloydsmith2005superspreading,james2007event} and has focused on models that link observed secondary case counts to a continuous random variable, the \emph{individual reproductive number}, via a Poisson sampling process. Canonically, the individual reproductive number is modeled as a gamma distribution, giving rise to negative binomial secondary case distributions \cite{lloydsmith2005superspreading}. In this way, variation arising from the stochastic transmission process is conceptually separated from \textit{intrinsic heterogeneity} in expected infectiousness that may emerge from potential differences in biology or behaviors between individuals \cite{chen2021heterogeneity, althouse2020superspreading,hamner2020high}.

Conventional models accounting for heterogeneity typically assume a constant pool of susceptible individuals and do not account for disease dynamics beyond the initial stages of an outbreak. Over longer timescales, susceptible depletion and replenishment drive population-scale disease dynamics. Changes in the size of susceptible population change the proportion of contacts that lead to successful disease transmission. This drives differences in expected cases between different cohorts of infectors. In practice, many deterministic epidemiological models also include \emph{explicit heterogeneity}, for example by adding compartments for different demographic or geographic groups \cite{schenzle1984agestructured}, which can have different average rates of mixing, transmission, or recovery. When implemented in terms of individual events, the inclusion of explicit heterogeneity reproduces superspreading in linear compartmental models \cite{meehan2023replicating}. Despite shared theory motivating models that highlight individual-level transmission, and those that emphasize population-level transmission dynamics, a gap persists in how each model framework treats individual-level transmission heterogeneity. 

The canonical SIR (Susceptible-Infected-Removed) model uses ordinary differential equations (ODEs) to describe disease dynamics. It assumes constant, uniform infectivity and recovery times across all individuals during the infectious stage \cite{Hethcote2000, Kermack1927}. Although it appears homogeneous, even the canonical SIR model has two sources of \emph{emergent heterogeneity.} The changing effective reproductive number arising from susceptible dynamics drives variation between fractions of the population infected at different times. Moreover, the exponentially distributed recovery times assumed by the ODE drive variation in the number of infections attributable to fractions of the population infected at a given time. In other words, even the fully deterministic SIR model predicts variation in the expected number of secondary cases.

Here we delineate sources of individual variation in transmission inscribed in simple dynamical models. We trace how these sources compose into observed or observable heterogeneity in different settings, for example contrasting how heterogeneity emerges in dynamical models versus linearized, or branching-process models. We begin by revisiting early findings that intrinsic heterogeneity (e.g., due to differences in the basic reproductive number, $\mathcal{R}_0$ ) drives differences in variation in the secondary case distribution. However, when accounting for the feedback of transmission on disease dynamics, we find via simulations and an analytical proof that emergent heterogeneity is an invariant across a full SIR outbreak and independent of differences in intrinsic heterogeneity. As a result, expected differences in superspreading may be a consequence, in part, of focusing on early rather than the full scope of outbreaks.  Throughout, we highlight the understudied but critically important role of disease dynamics \textit{per se} in generating individual-level heterogeneity. We aim to spur the development of theory and methods that better integrate heterogeneity and dynamics, ultimately facilitating feedback between data on incidence and individual-level transmission chains, mechanistic model specification, identification, validation, and intervention design.

\subsection{Heterogeneity in the secondary case distribution in the linearized SIR model}

The early outbreak phase of an epidemic is modeled using a linearized SIR model, which assumes that the pool of susceptible individuals does not change appreciably. Early dynamics can thus be characterized by a transmission rate and an infectious period. Demographic stochasticity generates heterogeneity in this model despite the lack of explicit accounting of individual-level variation in transmission rates and infectious periods. Since recovery is Markovian, the model implicitly assumes an exponentially distributed infectious period, resulting in an exponentially distributed expected infectiousness  (or \emph{individual reproductive number}) with mean $\mu = \mathcal{R}_0$ and dispersion $\kappa = 1$. The resulting distribution of secondary cases is geometric and has a mean equal to $\mathcal{R}_0$ (see Box 1). Thus, the expected and realized secondary-case distributions are characterized by $\mathcal{R}_0$ (\Fref{ls}a).

The heterogeneity in the secondary case distribution means that a large fraction of the initial secondary cases are generated by only a few infectors, even when starting from the canonical homogeneous assumption. The proportion of \emph{expected} cases caused by the individuals who remain infectious the longest is the same for any value of \Ro, but the proportion or \emph{realized} cases caused by the most effective infectors is larger for smaller values of \Ro, since the relative importance of the Poisson infection process increases (\Fref{ls}b). As $\mathcal{R}_0$ becomes smaller, an increasing fraction of infected individuals generate no secondary infections, so the individuals who do transmit make up a smaller fraction of all infectors, such that realized heterogeneity is expected to increase as $\mathcal{R}_0$ decreases.

\subsection{Heterogeneity in secondary cases over an epidemic is independent of $\mathcal{R}_0$ -- Proof}
We set out to explore the basis for the observation that both the mean and variance of secondary cases in the stochastically realized SIR model appear to be invariant with respect to changes in $\mathcal{R}_0$. To do so, consider the deterministic ODE-based SIR model:
\begin{align}
    \begin{split}
        \frac{dx}{dt} &= - \mathcal{R}_0 x(t)y(t), \\
        \frac{dy}{dt} &= \mathcal{R}_0 x(t)y(t) - y(t), \\
    \end{split}
\end{align}
where $x(t)$ is the proportion of susceptible individuals at time $t$, $y(t)$ is the proportion of infectious individuals at time $t$, $t$ is time measured in units of the mean infectious duration and $\mathcal{R}_0$ is the basic reproduction number.

To calculate the mean and variance of the secondary cases, we must track infections and the infectors that caused those infections. While individual infectors and cases cannot be tracked in this deterministic SIR model, we can track cohorts of infectors and the cases caused by them. By calculating the weighted moments of the secondary cases per case caused by each cohort, we can obtain the heterogeneity of the secondary cases per case over the epidemic. 

We define an “interval cohort”, $c_{\tau_1,\tau_2}$, as the fraction of infectors that become infectious at time $\tau_1$ and stop being infectious at time $\tau_2$. The weight of the cohort, $w(\tau_1,\tau_2)$ is the number of infectors in the cohort. The number of infectors who become infectious at time $\tau_1$ is $i(\tau_1) = \mathcal{R}_0 x(\tau_1)y(\tau_1)$. This quantity, $i(\tau_1)$ is also called the incidence of infection, or the number of new cases that appear at time $\tau_1$. Only a fraction of these infectors remain infectious till time $\tau_2$. If $f(.)$ denotes the probability density function of the infectious period, then
\begin{equation}
    w(\tau_1,\tau_2) = i(\tau_1)f(\tau_2-\tau_1)
\end{equation}

Now that we have the cohort weights, we need to calculate the number of cases per case caused by the cohort. The total number of cases caused by the cohort $c_{\tau_1,\tau_2}$ over the course of the epidemic is 
\begin{equation}
    \hat{C}(\tau_1, \tau_2) = \int_{\tau_1}^{\tau_2} \mathcal{R}_0 x(t') w(\tau_1,\tau_2)dt' = \mathcal{R}_0 w(\tau_1, \tau_2) \int_{\tau_1}^{\tau_2} x(t')dt'
\end{equation}
Then the secondary cases per case caused by cohort $c_{\tau_1,\tau_2}$ is
\begin{equation}
    C(\tau_1,\tau_2) = \frac{\hat{C}(\tau_1,\tau_2)}{w(\tau_1,\tau_2)} = \mathcal{R}_0 \int_{\tau_1}^{\tau_2} x(t')dt' 
\end{equation}

Now that we have the number of secondary cases per case and weight for each cohort, we can calculate the moments of the secondary cases per case distribution. The $k^{\text{th}}$ weighted raw moment of the secondary cases per case distribution, $C_k$ is
\begin{equation}
    C_k = \iint\limits_{0 < \tau_1 \leq \tau_2 < \infty}  d\tau_1 d\tau_2 \; w(\tau_1,\tau_2) (C(\tau_1 ,\tau_2))^k,
\end{equation}

With this definition of the raw moments, the assertion that the heterogeneity of the secondary case per case distribution over the entire epidemic is invariant is equivalent to:
\begin{equation}
    \kappa = \frac{C_0 C_2}{C_1^2} - 1 = 1 \label{eq:target_main}
\end{equation}
\jd{Took out the intuition for $C_1=C_0$. We already have it above, and it will disappoint some people who will start off think we're going to get to the interesting part of the result.}
We calculate the moments and confirm the result in section S1.1. We note that the result follows from two assumptions: the distribution of the infectious duration is memoryless, and the initial infectious cohort is infinitesimal, i.e., $y(t = 0) \rightarrow 0$. The first assumption is true for the canonical SIR model where the duration of infectious periods is exponentially distributed. We discuss relaxing the second assumption in section S1.2.

\subsection{Balance between within- and between-cohort components of the variance in secondary cases explains why heterogeneity in secondary cases is independent of $\mathcal{R}_0$}

To understand why the heterogeneity in secondary cases over the course of the epidemic is independent of $\mathcal{R}_0$, we divide the population into “forward cohorts” of individuals infected at the same time, and use variance partitioning to split the heterogeneity into two components: a within-cohort component and a between-cohort component. Individuals within a cohort have access to the same susceptible pool initially but have differences in their secondary case counts as a result of their exponentially distributed infectious periods. Thus, the within-cohort variance can be attributed to inherent individual-level differences in infectious periods. On the other hand, cohorts that arise at different times have realized differences in the number of secondary cases due to differences in the size of the susceptible pools that they have access to during the period in which the cohorts are active. It is important to note that both early and late cohorts have the same distributions of infectious periods and expected infectiousness but due to the depletion of the susceptible pool over time (\Fref{SIRsim}b), later cohorts, on average, have much lower realized secondary cases than earlier cohorts. Thus, the between-cohort variance can be attributed to susceptible depletion.

Numerical calculation of the within- and between-cohort components of the variance in secondary cases using the ODE-based SIR model (see methods for details of the calculation) shows that as $\mathcal{R}_0$ increases, the between-cohort component of the variance increases and the within-cohort component of the variances decreases (\Fref{SIRsim}c). However, the sum of the two components, remains constant across $\mathcal{R}_0$. As $\mathcal{R}_0$ increases, the variance in the size of infectious cohorts (\Fref{Cohort}a) and well as the speed and severity of susceptible depletion also increases (\Fref{SIRsim}b). As a result of the susceptible depletion, the mean number of secondary cases also decreases rapidly across cohorts (\Fref{Cohort}b), with earlier cohorts having a much higher mean of secondary cases compared to later cohorts. Further, the decline in the mean number of secondary cases across cohorts becomes sharper as $\mathcal{R}_0$ increases. As a result, the between-cohort variance in the number of secondary cases increases with $\mathcal{R}_0$.

The within-cohort variance, on the other hand, decreases as $\mathcal{R}_0$ increases (\Fref{SIRsim}c). Since the mean number of secondary cases varies widely between early and late cohorts, we look at the within-cohort dispersion in secondary cases to better understand how within-cohort heterogeneity changes between early vs late cohorts for an epidemic with a given $\mathcal{R}_0$. For a given $\mathcal{R}_0$, early cohorts have high dispersion in secondary cases, driven by access to a large susceptible pool (\Fref{Cohort}c). As the epidemic progresses, the susceptible pool depletes (\Fref{SIRsim}b) and the within-cohort dispersion in secondary cases decreases (\Fref{Cohort}c). The within-cohort dispersion increases for the later cohorts, driven by the shrinking within-cohort mean in the number of secondary cases (\Fref{Cohort}c). However, the within-cohort contribution to the total variance depends not just on the within-cohort dispersion, but also on the cohort weight. Once the contributions of each cohort to the variance are appropriately weighted by the cohort mean and cohort size, we find that the weighted within-cohort dispersion is low for both early and late cohorts, and is high for cohorts that arise in the middle of the epidemic (\Fref{Cohort}a) for a given $\mathcal{R}_0$. Indeed, across different values of $\mathcal{R}_0$, we find that the weighted within-cohort dispersion decreases as $\mathcal{R}_0$ increases (\Fref{SIRsim}c).

Together, the decrease in the within-cohort variances balances the increase in the between-cohort variance, leading to the total variances in secondary cases being independent of $\mathcal{R}_0$. This change in the within- and between-cohort variances is driven by susceptible depletion.

\section{Discussion}

\tg{From meeting notes: Focus on the bridge between individual based- vs population level perspectives. Even without explicit heterogeneity, SIR model has emergent heterogeneity due to disease dynamics. The invariance result is a special case resulting from the Markovian infection process. The result does not hold for other infectious period distributions but establishes a baseline for comparison. More work is needed.}

Quantifying heterogeneity in secondary-case distributions helps better understand how epidemics unfold and how they can be contained. Although there have been efforts to quantitatively describe individual-level heterogeneity in contact rates \cite{woolhouse1997heterogeneities} and to parameterize biologically realistic secondary case distributions, abstracted from the mechanism \cite{lloydsmith2005superspreading}, these efforts have been largely separate from the construction and evaluation of the compartmental models that enable epidemic forecasting and intervention design (but see \cite{meehan2023replicating}). Here, we expand the bridge between individual- and population perspectives of heterogeneity by studying epidemiologically-relevant cohorts of infectors that become infectious at the same time.

%% Restate results and interpret
We first show how demographic stochasticity and a Poisson contact process result in heterogeneity in secondary cases, even in the linearized SIR model with its canonical assumption of homogeneity between individuals, and lack of susceptible depletion. Next, we show how heterogeneity emerges in the SIR model of disease dynamics. Depletion of susceptibles over the course of an epidemic leads to differences in realized secondary cases between early and late infectors, despite sharing identical underlying infectiousness distributions. We prove a surprising result -- that the total variance in secondary cases over an epidemic is constant across all values of $\mathcal{R}_0$. This invariance is in contrast with the $\mathcal{R}_0$-dependent variance of secondary cases in the linearized SIR model. We show that the invariance arises due to a balance between the increase in between-cohort variance and the decrease in within-cohort variance, both driven by the dynamics of susceptible depletion. 

%%Implications and future directions 
Our cohort-level analyses reveal the importance of susceptible depletion, even in early stages of the epidemic. We find that even the earliest cohorts of infectors have mean secondary case counts much below the theoretical $\mathcal{R}_0$ (\Fref{Cohort}b). This discrepency becomes larger as $\mathcal{R}_0$ increases. In practice, this means that if surveillance begins after the initial transmission generations, mean secondary case counts will systematically underestimate $\mathcal{R}_0$ \cite{pellis}.
 
%% Caveats
The independence of the variance in secondary cases hinges upon the memorylessness of the infectious period distribution. This means that the result only holds for exponentially or geometically distributed infectious periods, which is only true in the SIR model. Further, the SIR model assumes a closed, well-mixed population. It is therefore, also unclear how heterogeneity in mixing propensities might impact our results. However, our results with the SIR model provide a baseline for comparing heterogeneity in secondary cases across different models. The results also reveal how the dispersion parameter, $\kappa$, can be used as a unified metric of heterogeneity across individual-based and compartmental models that we hope is of service in extending the current results to a richer set of model assumptions.
