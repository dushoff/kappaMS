\begin{abstract}
	Heterogeneity is a hallmark of infectious disease spread. It can arise from differences among individuals, their locations and their mixing patterns. This heterogeneity has consequences for disease dynamics, i.e., in the context of superspreading, where a large number of infections caused by a single infector can lead to an epidemic, despite a subcritical $\mathcal{R}_0$.
	As a result, these forms of heterogeneity and their consequences, especially in the invasion phase of epidemics, have been studied extensively. However, heterogeneity also arises from disease dynamics, wherein changes in the population of individuals with different infection statuses over the course of the epidemic, can lead to changes in the realized outcomes from underlying intrinsic variations. This form of heterogeneity has not been well studied.
	Here, we investigate this dynamics-based heterogeneity by analyzing the variability of secondary case distributions over the course of an SIR epidemic. We mathematically prove a surprising result -- the total variance in secondary cases, across the entire duration of the epidemic, is invariant across different values of $\mathcal{R}_0$. Further, this result holds broadly for epidemics where the distribution of infectious periods is memoryless. This total variance can be partitioned into two parts --a within-cohort component that arises from differences in the number of secondary cases produced by individuals infected at the same time and a between-cohort component that arises from differences in the mean number of secondary cases produced by cohorts across the duration of the epidemic. For epidemics with small $\mathcal{R}_0$, the within-cohort variation is large and is driven by underlying heterogeneities in infectious durations while the between-cohort variation is small since both early and later cohorts have access to similarly large pools of susceptible individuals. On the other hand, for epidemics with high $\mathcal{R}_0$, the within-cohort variation is small, since fast susceptible depletion reduces the realized variation that arises from the underlying variation in infectious durations, while the within-cohort variation is large, since early cohorts have access to a much larger pool of susceptibles than the later cohorts. The total variances in secondary cases is invariant to $\mathcal{R}_0$ as a result of this tradeoff between the within- and between-cohort variances across $\mathcal{R}_0$. We show that this tradeoff is driven by the dynamics of susceptible depletion, which is slow for low $\mathcal{R}_0$ and fast for high $\mathcal{R}_0$ epidemics. Our results demonstrate that heterogeneity emerges even in the simplest SIR epidemics as a result of disease dynamics and that susceptible depletion matters.
\end{abstract}
