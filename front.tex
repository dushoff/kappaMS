\begin{abstract}
	Heterogeneity is a hallmark of infectious disease spread. It is most prominent in the context of superspreading where a large number of secondary cases caused by a few infectors can lead to an outbreak despite a subcritical $\mathcal{R}_0$. This heterogeneity can arise from intrinsic individual-level differences in susceptibility, contact patterns and infectious durations, but also from the disease dynamics themselves; changes in the size of the susceptible pool over time lead to differences in realized outcomes despite identical underlying intrinsic heterogeneity. While intrinsic heterogeneity has been studied in the past, heterogeneity arising from disease dynamics has not been investigated. Here, we investigate this emergent heterogeneity by analyzing secondary case distributions for the canonical SIR model. We first describe $\kappa$, a statistic for quantifying the emergent heterogeneity in secondary case counts entailed in the SIR model and its stochastic realizations. We show that $\kappa$ is invariant across values of $\mathcal{R}_0$ using an individual based stochastic SIR model. Next, we prove this result mathematically, using the deterministic SIR model and identify conditions where this invariance holds. We show that this invariance ultimately results from susceptible depletion by analyzing the within- and between-cohort components of $\kappa$. Our results demonstrate that heterogeneity emerges even in simple SIR epidemics as a result of disease dynamics and that susceptible depletion drives variation in the secondary case distributions, both within and across cohorts. 
\end{abstract}
