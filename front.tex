\begin{abstract}
Heterogeneity in infectious disease spread can reshape the risk and severity of outbreaks. This heterogeneity can arise from intrinsic individual-level differences in susceptibility, contact patterns and infectious durations. Although intrinsic heterogeneity has been examined in depth, heterogeneity can also arise as a consequence of the disease dynamics. Changes in the size of susceptible populations can lead to emergent heterogeneity, i.e., differences in realized variation in the number of secondary cases caused by a primary case. Here, we investigate the magnitude and mechanisms generating emergent heterogeneity by analyzing secondary case distributions within the canonical SIR model. We first describe $\kappa$, a statistic for quantifying the emergent heterogeneity in secondary case counts in the SIR model and stochastic realizations. Although intrinsic heterogeneity decreases systematically with $\mathcal{R}_0$, we show that $\kappa$ is invariant for all values of $\mathcal{R}_0$ within stochastic SIR models and we prove this result mathematically. The invariance of emergent heterogeneity in SIR models results from distinct partitioning of within- and between-cohort components of $\kappa$. Our results demonstrate that heterogeneity emerges even in simple SIR epidemics as a result of feedback between stochastic depletion of susceptibles and subsequent opportunities of transmission. More broadly, our results suggest that efforts to understand the basis for realized variation in transmission should account for the impacts of susceptible depletion beyond the narrow limits of the initial outbreak phase.

 
\end{abstract}
